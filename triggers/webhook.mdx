A webhook trigger gives your script its own URL. When something sends a request to that URL, Script.it runs your script. Any service that can call a webhook — Zapier, Make, Stripe, your own internal tool — can start your automation this way, and can send data along with it. So can a form on your own website: see [Calling it from a web page](#calling-it-from-a-web-page).

## Creating a webhook trigger

<Steps>
  <Step title="Open the Triggers tab">
    Open the script you want to run, click the **Triggers** tab, then click **Add Trigger**.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Choose Webhook">
    Give it a name that says where the calls will come from — "Stripe payment received", "Deploy finished" — and choose **Webhook**.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Choose the script">
    Pick the script to run, and fill in any inputs it needs.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Create the trigger">
    Click **Create Trigger**. Script.it shows you the **webhook URL** and a **signing secret**, along with example code for sending a request. Copy them — you'll need them to set up the sending service.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Point your service at the URL">
    Paste the URL into whatever should call it. Most services have a **Send test event** button — use it, and check that the run shows up on your trigger.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Securing your webhook

Webhook triggers are **signed by default**. Script.it creates a signing secret when you make the trigger and checks every incoming request against it, so knowing the URL isn't enough to run your script.

The example code shown after you create the trigger already includes the signing, so you can copy it straight into your own service.

### Using a provider's own signing

If the calls come from a service that signs its own webhooks — Stripe, Slack, GitHub, Shopify, Zoom, HubSpot, Jira, Trello, Asana, Discord, Zendesk, Intercom, Linear, monday.com, SendGrid, Square, Paddle, Figma, Lemon Squeezy, WhatsApp — choose that provider on the trigger and paste in the secret it gives you. Script.it then checks requests the way that provider signs them, so you can point the service straight at your URL with nothing in between.

Some providers also do a one-time verification handshake when you add the URL. Script.it answers it for you, and shows you a verification token when a provider needs one pasted in.

### Rotating the secret

Clear the signing secret field and Script.it issues a new one. The URL stays the same, so you only have to update the sending service — no need to delete and rebuild the trigger.

<Warning>
  You can turn signature checking off if the service calling you can't sign its requests. If you do, the URL becomes the only thing protecting the trigger — treat it like a password. Don't share it publicly, commit it to a repository, or put it in client-side code. If it leaks, delete the trigger and create a new one to get a fresh URL. An **IP allowlist** on the trigger limits who can call it, for example to your office or VPN ranges.

  Some self-hosted installations don't allow webhooks without a signature. There, the setting can't be turned off, and a webhook that already has it off stops receiving until it's turned back on.
</Warning>

## Calling it from a web page

A form on your own website — a signup form, a contact form, a request form — can post straight to your webhook, and each submission runs your script. A web page can't keep a signing secret (anyone could read it in the page's source), so the form proves itself a different way: every submission carries a reCAPTCHA check, and only pages on the domains you allow count.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Allow your domain">
    Open the trigger's settings and enter your site's domain under **Allowed domains** — `example.com` covers `www.example.com` and every other subdomain. Add several separated by commas.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Turn on browser requests">
    Check **Allow browser requests**. Signature checking stays on for everything else that calls the URL.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Paste the form into your page">
    Open **Web page form** on the trigger and copy the code. It's a complete form with the webhook URL already filled in: rename the fields to what you want to collect and paste it into your page. It can appear more than once on a page.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Submit it once">
    Fill the form in on your live page and check that the run shows up on the trigger. Submissions from a page on any other domain are refused.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Or ask the agent — "add a signup form to acme.com that runs my welcome-email script" — and it sets the trigger up and hands you the form.

Every field in the form reaches your script by its name, and a field that appears more than once (a group of checkboxes) arrives as a list. The reCAPTCHA token never does — it's checked and removed before your script runs.

To keep a public form from being abused, each visitor can submit up to 30 times a minute, and a webhook takes up to 120 submissions a minute and 5,000 a day. Over those limits the form shows a "try again" message.

<Note>
  Nothing in the page can run your script without passing the check — not the URL on its own. On a self-hosted installation, the administrator decides whether a form can be used without the captcha on pages that aren't public, such as an intranet.
</Note>

## Sending data to your script

Whatever you send in the request body is handed to your script when it runs — a Stripe event, a GitHub push, the fields of a web page form, or any JSON you choose. Your script can read those values and act on them.

## What happens when the webhook fires

Script.it accepts the request straight away and runs your script right after, so a slow script never causes a timeout on the sending side.

Runs appear in your session history like any other run, and in the trigger's run history on the script. See [Managing triggers](/triggers/managing).

## Managing webhook triggers

- **Turn it off and on** from the script. The URL is kept, so turning it back on resumes with the same setup.
- **Delete it** to permanently invalidate the URL. Anything still calling it stops working, and a new trigger gets a different URL.

<Note>
  If you want to react to something in an app you've already connected — a Slack message, a GitHub pull request, a Stripe payment — you usually don't need a webhook trigger at all. Use an [app event](/triggers/app-events) instead and skip the setup.
</Note>
